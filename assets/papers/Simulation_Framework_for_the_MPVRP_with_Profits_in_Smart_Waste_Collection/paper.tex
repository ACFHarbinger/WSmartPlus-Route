\begin{abstract}
Smart waste collection is a stochastic multi-period routing problem: bins fill
between decisions, sensing is imperfect, and collecting a bin today changes the
opportunities available tomorrow. We introduce WSmartRoute+, a Python
simulation framework for evaluating policies for this setting. Each policy
combines interchangeable mandatory-bin selection, profitable route construction,
and route-improvement stages. This structure allows one decision to vary while
the demand realisation and other stages remain fixed. The reported
benchmark crosses five selection variants, eight classical constructors, two
improvers, two waste-generation processes, and three municipal network sizes at
a 30-day horizon. The results show a substantially larger observed efficiency
spread across selection variants than across constructors, exposing a clear
trade-off between collection efficiency and overflow risk. Matched-demand
improver configurations differ substantially, although stochastic upstream
construction prevents causal attribution to that stage. A selected subset is
followed for 90 days. We report matched within-policy comparisons only because
selection on 30-day performance precludes an unbiased cross-constructor ranking.
Learned constructors share the framework interface but are not benchmarked here.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Waste collection routing couples transport cost to service reliability. Bins
accumulate material at different rates, sensor readings are imperfect, vehicle
capacity is limited, and a collection changes the following day's state. A
useful evaluation must therefore follow a policy over time instead of scoring an
isolated route.

Existing work covers exact models, meta-heuristics, sensor placement, and
data-driven planning for waste collection
\cite{hess2024waste,jorge2022hybrid,lopes2023efficient,de2024data}. Neural
combinatorial optimization (NCO) adds learned constructive and improvement
methods for routing \cite{wu2024neural}. Results remain difficult to compare
when studies use different operational dynamics, demand samples, or tightly
coupled selection and routing rules.

WSmartRoute+ provides a simulation layer for the stochastic MPVRPP. It
models daily accumulation, noisy sensing, route execution, overflow and loss,
then carries the resulting state into the next decision. Policy components use
registries and a common interface, allowing classical or learned constructors
to be combined with the same selection and improvement stages.

We evaluate the classical baseline on bin locations, road distances, and
historical fill records from two Portuguese municipalities. The complete
30-day factorial design contains 480 runs. A performance-selected subset is
also observed for 90 days, with the corresponding inferential limits stated
explicitly.

This paper makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A reproducible simulator for stochastic multi-period waste collection,
    including bin-level accumulation, sensing error, overflow, loss, and state
    transitions after collection.
    
    \item A three-stage policy interface that separates mandatory selection,
    profitable route construction, and route improvement, with common demand
    realisations for matched comparisons.
    
    \item A classical baseline spanning five selection variants, eight
    constructors, two improvers, two demand processes, and three municipal
    network sizes; learned constructors can use the same interface in future
    comparisons.
    
    \item An integrity-aware analysis that excludes failed runs by whole
    scenario cells, balances every marginal comparison, and limits the selected
    90-day follow-up to claims supported by its design.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem Definition}
We model the Multi-Period Vehicle Routing Problem with Profits (MPVRPP) as a
constrained stochastic optimization problem over $D$ days.

\subsection{Problem State and Dynamics}
Let $\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})$ be a complete graph with depot
$v_0$ and bins $\mathcal{V}\setminus\{v_0\}$. Bin $i$ has location
$\vec{l}_i\in\mathbb{R}^2$, capacity $C_i$, and waste level
$w_{i,d}\in[0,C_i]$ on day $d$. The system state is
$\vec{w}_d=[w_{1,d},\ldots,w_{V,d}]^\top$.

Daily accumulation $\delta_{i,d}\sim P_i$ is bin-specific and stochastic. If
bin $i$ is collected on day $d$, the next day's level contains only the next
increment; otherwise accumulation continues to capacity:
\begin{equation}
    w_{i, d+1} = 
    \begin{cases} 
        \delta_{i, d+1} & \text{if } v_i \in \mathcal{A}_d \\
        \min(w_{i, d} + \delta_{i, d+1}, C_i) & \text{if } v_i \notin \mathcal{A}_d
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Optimization Objective}

On day $d$, the policy produces up to $K$ routes. Route $k$ visits
$T_{d,k}$ bins and starts and ends at $v_0$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{A}_{d,k}=(a_{0,k},a_{1,k},\ldots,a_{T_{d,k},k},a_{T_{d,k}+1,k}),
    \qquad a_{0,k}=a_{T_{d,k}+1,k}=v_0.
\end{equation}

Let $\mathcal{A}_{d,\cdot}$ denote the union of non-depot visits. The daily
problem is

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \textrm{maximize} \quad & \mathcal{P}_d = r_w \sum_{v_i\in\mathcal{A}_{d,\cdot}}w_{i,d}
    -c_{km}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=0}^{T_{d,k}}
    \dist(a_{t,k},a_{t+1,k}) \label{eq:profit_function}\\
    \textrm{subject to} \quad
    & a_{t,k}\neq a_{t',k'} \quad
      \forall (t,k)\neq(t',k'),\ 1\leq t\leq T_{d,k},\ 1\leq t'\leq T_{d,k'},\\
    & \sum_{t=1}^{T_{d,k}}w_{a_{t,k},d}\leq Q_k
      \quad \forall k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $r_w$ is revenue per kilogram, $c_{km}$ is travel cost per kilometre, and
$Q_k$ is vehicle $k$'s capacity. Optional service distinguishes the VRPP from
the classical VRP: the policy chooses a profitable subset as well as its routes
\cite{doi:10.1137/1.9781611973594.ch10}.

Because each collection changes the next state, policy $\bm{\pi}$ maximises
expected cumulative profit over the horizon:
\begin{equation}
    \max_{\bm{\pi}} \mathbb{E} \left[ \sum_{d=1}^D \mathcal{P}_d(\mathcal{A}_{d,\cdot}, \bm{w}_d) \right],
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}_d$ is defined in \eqref{eq:profit_function}.



\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Neural Combinatorial Optimization}
Learned solvers fit the framework at either route construction or improvement;
the present experiment does not benchmark them. Constructive solvers may decode
a route sequentially, as in Pointer Networks, the Attention Model, and POMO
\cite{vinyals2015pointer,Kool2018AttentionLT,kwon2020pomo}, or predict edge
scores or a complete solution representation for a separate decoder
\cite{joshi2019efficient,karalias2020erdos,sun2023difusco}. Learning-to-search
methods instead refine an existing solution through learned local moves
\cite{ma2024learning}. These families use supervised, reinforcement, or
unsupervised objectives and differ materially in inference cost and
generalisation beyond their training sizes
\cite{joshi2020learning,wu2024neural}. The shared constructor interface permits
a later controlled comparison with the classical baseline reported here.

\subsection{Multi-Period Routing with Profits}
Classical VRP formulations require service at every customer, whereas profitable
routing jointly chooses whom to serve and how to route the chosen subset
\cite{doi:10.1137/1.9781611973594.ch10}. Prior multi-period profitable routing
distributes service across periods under inter-period scheduling constraints
\cite{DBLP:journals/eor/ZhangCCLQ13}. Waste collection introduces a different
coupling: service resets a bin's state, while deferral raises its future
collection value and overflow risk. The policy must trade immediate routing cost
against the future system state.
Waste-routing studies have developed exact and heuristic methods for realistic
operational constraints \cite{hess2024waste,jorge2022hybrid,lopes2023efficient,de2024data},
but reproducible comparisons across selection, construction, and improvement
rules remain difficult when each study embeds those decisions in a monolithic
solver. WSmartRoute+ addresses that evaluation gap by separating the decisions
and replaying identical stochastic demand across composed policies.



\section{Methodology}

A WSmartRoute+ policy composes three independent stages, each drawn from its
own plugin registry
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pol_config}). The first is \emph{mandatory selection}: given the
sensed fill level of every bin, decide which bins \emph{must} be serviced today.
The second is \emph{route construction}: build a route over those mandatory bins,
free to add any further bin whose waste repays the detour needed to reach it.
The third, optional, stage is \emph{route improvement}: take the constructed
route and refine it.

The stages answer different questions. Selection is multi-period because
collecting today forgoes a larger, cheaper-per-kilogram load tomorrow.
Construction solves the current profitable-routing problem, and improvement
refines its geometry. Their composition lets an experiment vary one stage while
holding the others fixed. Registered implementations therefore define a policy
\emph{space} instead of one fixed solver.

This work benchmarks a deliberately small, representative subset of that space:
three selection strategies (in five threshold variants), eight route constructors
spanning exact methods, meta-heuristics and hyper-heuristics, and two route
improvers.

\subsection{Mandatory Selection Strategies}

All three strategies decide from \emph{sensed} fill levels, never from the true
ones, and all three must estimate whatever they need about accumulation from the
history the simulation has produced so far. Neither the generating distribution
nor its parameters are ever revealed to them. The three were chosen to span the
conceptual space rather than to be exhaustive: one reactive, one statistical,
one simulation-based.

\paragraph{Last-Minute (LM).}
The simplest possible rule, and the reactive baseline: a bin becomes mandatory
when its sensed fill level crosses a fixed critical-fill threshold, judged in
complete isolation from every other bin and from any estimate of how fast it is
filling. Formally, bin $i$ is mandatory on day $t$ iff $\hat{f}_{i,t} \geq \tau$,
for sensed fill $\hat{f}_{i,t}$ and threshold $\tau$. We test $\tau = 0.7$
(CF70) and $\tau = 0.9$ (CF90). The threshold is the only knob, and it trades
overflow risk against visit frequency directly, with no model connecting the two.

\paragraph{Service-Level (SL).}
A statistical rule that projects forward rather than reacting. For each bin it
maintains online estimates of the mean daily accumulation $\hat{\mu}_i$ and its
standard deviation $\hat{\sigma}_i$ from the days observed so far, and flags bin
$i$ as mandatory when a conservative projection of its fill $h$ days ahead
already exceeds capacity $C_i$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{f}_{i,t} \cdot C_i + h\hat{\mu}_i + z\hat{\sigma}_i\sqrt{h} \;\geq\; C_i ,
\end{equation}
where $z$ is a fixed safety coefficient. We test projection horizons $h=1$ (SL1)
and $h=2$ (SL2), holding $z$ constant, so the two variants differ in how far
ahead they look and not in how conservative they are at a given distance.

Two caveats apply to reading this as a probabilistic guarantee. The safety
margin approximates a Gaussian quantile, whereas the waste actually generated in
our scenarios is right-skewed; and $\hat{\mu}_i$, $\hat{\sigma}_i$ are cold-start
estimates that are least reliable early in a horizon, exactly when the strategy
has had least opportunity to recover from a mistake.

\paragraph{Look-Ahead (LA).}
A simulation-based rule that assumes no distributional form at all. Rather than
projecting analytically, it rolls the bin's accumulation forward day by day to
find the last day on which the bin could still be collected without overflowing,
and makes it mandatory on that day. This defers collection to the latest safe
moment so that the collected load is as large as possible. It has no
threshold variant, which is why it contributes fewer configurations than LM or
SL to the experimental grid. It is also the most expensive of the three per
decision, since it simulates forward instead of evaluating a closed form.

\subsection{Route Constructors}

Every constructor below solves the same single-period problem: visit all
mandatory bins, optionally visit any subset of the remaining bins, respect
vehicle capacity, and maximise collected waste net of travel cost. The
adaptation is uniform across the families. Classical CVRP methods assume every
node must be visited; each constructor therefore needs a mechanism to reject
bins whose collected value would not repay the detour.

\subsubsection{Exact Methods}

\paragraph{Smart Waste Collection: Two-Commodity Flow (SWC-TCF).}
A direct implementation of the waste-collection formulation of Ramos et
al.~\cite{RAMOS2018146}, written as a single monolithic mixed-integer program
and handed to a general-purpose solver. Its core device is to track two
quantities on every arc simultaneously: waste collected so far and residual
capacity. Their sum equals vehicle capacity on every used arc. This constraint
family enforces capacity and eliminates subtours at
once, where a conventional formulation needs two separate and awkward families.

Unlike the other constructors, SWC-TCF needs no VRPP retrofit: the source
formulation already treats ``is bin $i$ visited'' as a first-class binary, with
the objective trading collection revenue against travel cost and a per-vehicle
usage penalty directly. Bins flagged mandatory, or already critically full, are
forced into the solution regardless.

Its exposure is scale. The model grows roughly quadratically in the number of
candidate bins and there is no decomposition to keep the working problem small,
making it the constructor most vulnerable to instance-size blow-up. The largest
scenario exhibits this vulnerability
(Sect.~\ref{sec:integrity}).

\paragraph{Branch-Price-and-Cut (BPC).}
The decomposition-based exact method. The master problem is a route-selection
linear program in which each column is one complete candidate route; routes are
generated on demand by a pricing subproblem rather than enumerated, since the
number of possible routes is astronomically large for any non-trivial instance.
Pricing solves a resource-constrained shortest-path problem whose arc costs
incorporate the master problem's current duals, so a route is only generated if
it could improve the incumbent. Because an exactly elementary path search is
itself intractable at this scale, the pricer works with near-elementary paths and
tightens that relaxation adaptively when generated routes begin revisiting bins
too often.

Three mechanisms together give BPC its profitable-subset behaviour. Coverage
constraints are \emph{at most once} for optional bins and \emph{exactly once} for
mandatory ones; the pricing subproblem maximises profit rather than minimising
distance, so route extension stops naturally once a detour costs more than the
waste it collects; and branching resolves the visit/no-visit decision for a bin
\emph{before} it branches on arcs, settling the question a classical
always-cover formulation never has to ask. Beyond the base formulation, lifted
cover inequalities on saturated arcs are separated, following the reference
methodology, together with VRPP-specific minimum-cut, triangle-clique and
node-profit families.

Both exact methods run under a per-run time budget in these experiments. Neither
is run to certified optimality on the larger instances, and both have a
documented non-exact fallback on timeout: BPC returns a greedily constructed
plan, SWC-TCF its best incumbent. Their results should be read as ``best
solution found within budget'', not as proven optima.

\subsubsection{Meta-Heuristics}

\paragraph{Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search (ALNS).}
A trajectory method that maintains one incumbent and repeatedly destroys and
repairs part of it. Its adaptivity is in operator choice: it holds a portfolio of
destroy operators (random removal, worst removal, and Shaw removal, which
removes a set of mutually related bins so that repair has the freedom to place
them jointly) and repair operators (greedy insertion and regret-$k$ insertion,
optionally with randomised noise on insertion costs), and reweights them by
recent success. Operator $i$ is drawn by roulette wheel from weights updated
every $\varphi$ iterations:
\begin{equation}
    w_{i,t+1} = \lambda w_{i,t} + (1-\lambda)\frac{\pi_i}{\theta_i},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda \in [0,1]$ is a decay factor, $\pi_i$ the score accumulated by
operator $i$ during the segment, and $\theta_i$ its usage count. Candidates are
accepted by a simulated-annealing rule, so the trajectory can escape local
optima. The profitable-subset decision falls out of repair: a removed bin for
which no insertion position yields positive marginal profit is simply left out.

\paragraph{Hybrid Genetic Search (HGS).}
A memetic algorithm following Vidal~\cite{VIDAL2022105643}, and the population
counterpart to ALNS. Its central design choice is representational: individuals
are encoded as a single giant tour over all bins with no route boundaries, and
are decoded into actual routes only when evaluated. Decoding is a dynamic
program that finds the optimal split of the giant tour into capacity-feasible
segments. For the VRPP, decoding can also skip any bin whose inclusion reduces net
profit. Skipped bins remain in the genotype, so their relative position survives
into later generations in case a subsequent mutation makes them worth collecting
again.

Two sub-populations are maintained, one feasible and one infeasible-but-penalised,
so that promising infeasible individuals survive long enough to be repaired.
Each generation selects two parents by a tournament biased toward both fitness
and diversity, recombines them, and educates the offspring by local search over
relocate, 2-opt and the algorithm's signature \textsc{swap*} move, which searches
for each exchanged node's genuinely best new position rather than only the
partner's vacated slot, pruned to angularly nearby sectors to stay tractable.
Fitness combines cost $C(S)$ with a diversity contribution $D(S)$:
\begin{equation}
    F(S) = C(S) + \left(1 - \frac{|P_{feas}|}{|P|}\right) \cdot D(S).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Simulated Annealing Neighborhood Search (SANS).}
An implementation of the hybrid published by Jorge et
al.~\cite{jorge2022hybrid} specifically for smart waste collection. It couples
the standard annealing acceptance rule, which always accepts improvements and
accepts a worsening move of magnitude $\Delta$ with probability $\exp(-\Delta/T)$
under a cooling temperature $T$. It combines this rule with a purpose-built move
library: single-bin relocation and swapping within and between routes, batched
relocation and swapping of both random and contiguous bin groups, segment
reversal, and route-segment crossover. Moves are sampled uniformly rather than
adaptively, which is the substantive difference from ALNS: SANS steers entirely
through acceptance, never down-weighting an unproductive operator.

The profitable-subset decision is folded into the neighbourhood itself. Bins can
be moved into a removed pool and reconsidered for reinsertion later, so
inclusion is one more move type rather than a separate phase. A refinement stage
follows the annealing loop: an uncrossing pass, further annealing, deterministic
local search applied in forward order and then with routes and positions
reversed, followed by a final pass over the removed pool. Reversing the sweep
can reach moves hidden by the first order.

\paragraph{Pheromone-Guided Cooperative Large Neighborhood Search (PG-CLNS).}
An original design combining population-level construction with per-individual
refinement. Construction is ant-colony-based, with pheromone deposited on
\emph{edges} of the bin graph, so that road segments appearing in good solutions
are more likely to be chosen again; only the global best solution deposits into
the shared trail each round, keeping the signal focused, while a temporary
within-round penalty discourages ants from reusing edges another ant has just
taken. Refinement is large neighbourhood search, run independently on every
member of the population with its own adaptive destroy/repair weighting.

The two levels adapt on different objects and at different scopes: pheromone is
edge-based and population-wide, operator weights are move-based and per
individual. Each round constructs a population under the current trail, refines
every member, updates the trail from the round's best, and regenerates the
weakest fraction of the population under the updated field. Profitability is
screened at insertion in both phases, as in ALNS.

Because every member is refined every round, total work scales with population
size times refinement depth times rounds; on non-trivial instances the algorithm
is typically stopped by its time budget rather than by convergence.

\paragraph{Particle Swarm Optimization Memetic Algorithm (PSOMA).}
Particle swarm optimisation is a continuous method, and applying it to a
sequencing problem requires an encoding. PSOMA uses ranked-order values: each
particle holds a real vector with one coordinate per bin, and the visiting order
is simply the rank order of those coordinates. The numbers themselves are
meaningless; only their relative order is read, so the standard continuous
velocity update combines inertia with attraction to the particle's own best and
the swarm's best. Sorting the updated values yields the discrete tour. The
resulting giant tour is split into routes by the same
profit-aware dynamic program HGS uses, which is likewise where PSOMA decides
which bins to skip.

The memetic component refines the swarm's incumbent once per iteration by a
simulated-annealing local search over swap, relocate and segment-reversal moves,
adaptively favouring whichever move type has recently been productive. A
secondary heuristic biases the local search's cost estimates so that pairs of
profitable bins appear closer together, steering cheap moves toward retaining
valuable bins without paying for a full route decode.

\subsubsection{Hyper-Heuristics}

\paragraph{Ant Colony Optimization Hyper-Heuristic (ACO-HH).}
The one true hyper-heuristic in the benchmark, and worth contrasting directly
with PG-CLNS, which uses the same metaphor one level lower. Where classical ACO
lays pheromone on edges of the \emph{bin} graph to decide which bin to visit
next, ACO-HH lays pheromone on transitions in a graph whose nodes are the
available low-level heuristics, and so decides which \emph{move to apply} next.
It searches the space of operator sequences and delegates all solution
modification to the operator it selects.

An ant walks this operator graph, applying each move it steps onto to the working
solution. The next operator is drawn with probability
\begin{equation}
    p_{sh} = \frac{[\tau_{sh}]^{\alpha}[\eta_{sh}]^{\beta}}
                  {\sum_{k \in \mathcal{H}} [\tau_{sk}]^{\alpha}[\eta_{sk}]^{\beta}},
\end{equation}
where $\tau_{sh}$ is the pheromone on the transition from state $s$ to heuristic
$h$, $\eta_{sh}$ is heuristic information about $h$'s likely efficacy, and
$\alpha,\beta$ weight the two. If a completed walk improved the solution,
pheromone is deposited along every transition it used, so operator
\emph{sequences} that work well together are reinforced rather than merely
individually good operators; a faster-adapting efficiency signal that accounts
for each operator's runtime modulates selection on a shorter timescale, and all
pheromone evaporates so that stale sequences fade.

Two separate portfolios are involved: construction heuristics (greedy,
nearest- and farthest-insertion, regret-based, each with a profit-aware variant)
used once to build the initial solution, and the modification operators the ant
colony actually searches over. Inclusion decisions are handled by the
profit-aware variants throughout, supplemented by a strategic-oscillation
mechanism that periodically relaxes the capacity penalty when the search
stagnates, letting it revisit inclusion decisions it would otherwise be trapped
away from.

\subsection{Route Improvers}

Both improvers take a constructed route plan and refine it, and neither is
designed to change which bins were selected.

\paragraph{Classical Local Search (CLS).}
Variable-neighbourhood descent over a fixed toolbox ordered from cheapest to
most expensive to evaluate: relocate a bin, swap two bins, reverse a segment
(2-opt), relocate a chain of two or three consecutive bins (Or-opt), exchange
route tails between vehicles, exchange bins between routes with re-optimised
placement, and finally full three- and four-bin reordering. Whenever any move
succeeds the search restarts from the cheapest neighbourhood rather than
continuing, and it terminates at a local optimum with respect to every move in
the toolbox.

One property matters for interpreting the results: every CLS move is scored
purely by its effect on travel distance. Capacity is enforced as a feasibility
check, but collected revenue never enters the accept/reject decision, so CLS
optimises distance and not profit. It can, however, move a bin between vehicles.

\paragraph{Fast-TSP.}
A narrower operation: holding each route's bin assignment fixed, find the best
visiting order within it. Routes of up to roughly twenty stops are solved to
optimality by dynamic programming; longer ones fall back to a randomised local
search within a small fixed budget. Fast-TSP cannot move a bin between vehicles,
drop a bin, or account for revenue. It solves each route's ordering globally,
whereas CLS improves the plan incrementally. Section~\ref{sec:res-improvers}
compares configurations using the two improvers.

\subsection{Simulation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Each simulated day executes a fixed cycle: bins accumulate waste; the selection
strategy reads sensed fill levels and flags mandatory bins; the constructor
builds a route and the improver refines it; the route is executed and the
collected waste recorded; the day is logged and the state carried forward.

Four properties of this loop bear on how the results should be read.

\emph{Comparisons are paired at the level of the demand realisation.} The
simulator re-seeds its waste generation per policy-and-day combination rather
than letting each run consume randomness in its own order, so every algorithm in
a given scenario faces the literal identical sequence of daily fill levels, bin
by bin and day by day. Differences between algorithms in the same scenario are
therefore differences in decision-making, not in the draws they happened to face.
This also underwrites the integrity check of Sect.~\ref{sec:integrity}: within a
scenario the total waste available to collect is fixed, so collected tonnage is
directly comparable across constructors.

\emph{Selection acts on noisy observations.} Strategies see a sensed fill level
perturbed by random error; overflow and loss accounting always uses the true
level. A strategy can therefore make a wrong call purely from what its sensors
reported, as in a real deployment.

\emph{Overflow is a flag, loss is a quantity.} A bin's true level is tracked
continuously; waste beyond capacity is recorded as lost and the displayed level
capped. An overflowing bin that stays unvisited keeps losing each subsequent
increment until collection. Overflow counts and kilograms lost are therefore related but distinct
measures of service failure.

\emph{Every scenario uses a single vehicle and a single depot.} This is a
deliberate scope restriction and a real limitation: multi-vehicle and multi-depot
dispatch is not exercised anywhere in these experiments, and the capacity
constraint is delegated to each constructor's own internal mechanism rather than
policed centrally by the simulator.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Scenarios and Data}

Experiments use two Portuguese municipal networks with observed bin locations
and road-distance matrices:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rio Maior}, at $N = 100$ and $N = 170$ plastic-waste bins,
          with road distances from the Google Maps distance matrix;
    \item \textbf{Figueira da Foz}, at $N = 350$ bins, with road distances from
          OpenStreetMap.
\end{itemize}

Two waste-generation processes drive each network, chosen to contrast a
parametric model against real observations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gamma-3}, drawing daily accumulation from fitted Gamma
          distributions ($\alpha \in \{1,3\}$, $\beta \in \{8,6\}$; means 8, 6,
          24 and 18\,kg/day with variances 64, 36, 192 and 108) with different
          presets assigned to different groups of bins, so accumulation
          behaviour varies across the network and not merely in scale;
    \item \textbf{Empirical}, resampling directly from historical daily-fill
          records collected by the WSmart+ Bin Analysis module between
          2021-01-12 and 2023-10-23. This bootstrap makes no parametric
          assumption and replays observed patterns.
\end{itemize}

Neither process reveals its parameters to any selection strategy; all
accumulation statistics a strategy uses are estimated online from the days it
has observed (Sect.~\ref{sec:protocol}). Two planning horizons are used, 30 and
90 days. The two networks are shown side by side in Fig.~\ref{fig:networks}.

Both share a feature that shapes every result reported later: the depot is
remote from the bins it serves. Its median road distance to a bin is roughly
five times the median distance between two bins (52.9 against 8.6 units at Rio
Maior, 46.6 against 10.6 at Figueira da Foz), so every route pays a long
outbound and return leg before it collects anything. Selection strategies that
collect on fewer days therefore amortise that fixed cost over a larger load,
which is the mechanism behind the efficiency ordering in
Sect.~\ref{sec:res-strategies}.

\subsection{Design of Experiments}

The 30-day design is a complete factorial crossing of every policy stage with
every scenario:
\[
    \underbrace{8}_{\text{constructors}} \times
    \underbrace{5}_{\text{selection variants}} \times
    \underbrace{2}_{\text{improvers}} \times
    \underbrace{2}_{\text{distributions}} \times
    \underbrace{3}_{\text{network sizes}}
\]
yielding 480 simulation runs, balanced so that each constructor appears in every
scenario an equal number of times. Because the demand realisation is fixed per
scenario (Sect.~\ref{sec:protocol}), the marginal mean of any one stage is a
genuine like-for-like comparison rather than a comparison confounded by which
scenarios that stage happened to be run in.

The 90-day design is \emph{not} a factorial replication of this, and the
distinction is important enough to state before any 90-day number is read. Only
policy configurations that lay on the 30-day Pareto front were carried forward,
producing 174 runs. Each constructor's 90-day rows are therefore the scenarios
in which it had already performed well at 30 days, and a cross-constructor
comparison at 90 days would compare each constructor against a different,
self-favouring subset. We report no such comparison. The horizon analysis in
Sect.~\ref{sec:res-horizon} instead pairs each configuration against
\emph{itself} across the two horizons.

We report five measures. \emph{Efficiency} (kg/km) is collected waste per
kilometre driven and is the primary operational objective. \emph{Overflow
events} counts bin-days on which a bin was at capacity, and \emph{kilograms
lost} the waste discarded as a result; the two are related but distinct, since a
single bin left uncollected keeps losing waste every subsequent day.
\emph{Distance} and \emph{runtime} measure operational and computational cost.
Means are reported alongside medians because a single extreme scenario can move
a mean substantially at this sample size (Sect.~\ref{sec:integrity}).

\subsection{Data Integrity}
\label{sec:integrity}

Four runs are degenerate rather than merely poor, and all four are SWC-TCF on
Figueira da Foz under Gamma-3 (Table~\ref{tab:excluded}). Because every
constructor in a scenario collects from an identical waste realisation,
collected tonnage is comparable by construction. These runs collected between
30\% and 86\% less than their scenario median and failed to complete. Their very
large overflow counts include 23{,}886 events for the 90-day case, which reached
day 13 of 90, and principally reflect truncation. This is the concrete realisation of the
scaling exposure noted for the monolithic MILP formulation: it is the largest
network under the heavier of the two generating processes.

The detection rule is a tonnage shortfall against the scenario median, and its
threshold is not tuned. Outside these four runs the shortfall distribution has
median $0.00$ and does not exceed $0.071$; the four sit at $0.30$, $0.47$,
$0.47$ and $0.86$. The gap identifies them unambiguously. Collection-day count
cannot diagnose truncation: several strong policies collect on only 15 of 30
days because they bundle collections well. Tonnage provides the relevant signal.

Excluding these runs alone would have introduced a subtler bias of the same
family as the 90-day sampling problem: since all four are SWC-TCF's, removing
only them would average SWC-TCF over the scenarios where it did not fail while
averaging its rivals over those scenarios too. We therefore drop the affected
scenario cells for \emph{all} constructors, at a cost of 23 further runs,
leaving 456 runs at 30 days with every constructor contributing exactly 57.

The same reasoning applies to every other marginal comparison, in a form that is
easy to miss. Dropping a scenario cell removes all constructors together, but it
removes only \emph{one} selection variant and \emph{one} improver, since those
identify the cell. Marginal tables over those stages are therefore additionally
restricted to the slices in which every level of the compared stage actually
ran. All tables and figures in this section are generated from the simulation
output by a single tool that applies these rules centrally, so no reported
number is transcribed by hand and the paper cannot drift from the data.

\subsection{Results}

\subsubsection{Route Constructors}
\label{sec:res-constructors}

Table~\ref{tab:constructors30} reports per-constructor performance, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto} places the constructors in the efficiency/service plane.

BPC attains the highest mean efficiency (6.65\,kg/km) on the shortest mean
routes (4{,}018\,km), although its time limit prevents an optimality certificate
at these sizes. ALNS achieves the fewest mean
overflows (5.9) and PG-CLNS is the strongest all-round compromise, second on
efficiency and second on overflows. ACO-HH ranks third on efficiency, within
5\% of BPC, and is the fastest constructor at 589\,s mean runtime, roughly a
quarter of the HGS and PG-CLNS means.

Seven of the eight constructors have a median overflow count of 4.0. The spread
in their means, from 5.9 for ALNS to 19.9 for HGS, is therefore driven largely
by tail events. Constructor differences on this measure describe the severity
of unusual cases more than typical performance.

Pareto-front membership across the six scenario--distribution combinations,
recomputed after the integrity filter, gives PG-CLNS 5 of 6, PSOMA and HGS 3
each, BPC 2, and ACO-HH and ALNS 1 each. PG-CLNS is thus the most consistently
non-dominated constructor even though it never leads a single column outright,
which is the characteristic signature of a good compromise method.

Runtime separates the constructors more cleanly than quality does
(Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}). At $N=350$ the spread is 4.5-fold, from ACO-HH at
1{,}219\,s to SANS at 5{,}451\,s. BPC averages 2{,}430\,s; PG-CLNS, HGS and
SANS all require more wall-clock time per run at this scale.

\subsubsection{Mandatory Selection Strategies}
\label{sec:res-strategies}

The selection stage produces a substantially larger observed spread than the
constructor stage (Table~\ref{tab:strategies30}, Fig.~\ref{fig:strategy}). The
range in mean efficiency across the five tested selection variants is nearly
four times the corresponding range across constructors. A benchmark that fixed
selection and varied only construction would miss the larger operational
trade-off in this design. The comparison describes the tested levels and does
not constitute a general variance decomposition.

The five variants order monotonically on every reported measure: efficiency and
overflow events fall together, while distance and runtime rise. Across these
three different selection mechanisms, the observed ordering behaves like a
single operational dial: deferring collection hauls more per kilometre on
shorter routes but permits more overflows. No tested variant dominates another,
so deployment requires an explicit valuation of service failure against travel.

At similar service levels, the mechanisms carry different distance costs.
Look-Ahead attains 5.5 mean overflows at 3{,}396\,km, while Service-Level (SL1)
needs 4{,}147\,km to reach 3.3. The marginal cost of those last two overflow
events is roughly 750\,km, an order of magnitude greater than the 456       \,km that
buys the nine-event reduction between LM-CF90 and Look-Ahead. These observed
increments indicate sharply diminishing service returns.

The mechanism behind the dial is visible at the level of a single bin
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fill}). Because the demand realisation is fixed, the same bin
faces the identical waste increments under every strategy; what changes is when
it is collected. Under the CF70 threshold the bin is emptied early and often,
so its level never approaches capacity. Under CF90 collection is deferred until
the bin is nearly full, hauling more waste per visit but leaving the bin one bad
day from overflow. The highest-accumulation bins do overflow. The aggregate
trade-off of Fig.~\ref{fig:strategy} is this dynamic
summed over every bin and day.

overflows in the unrestricted data and performs worse in the balanced data.

\subsubsection{Route Improvers}
\label{sec:res-improvers}

The two improvers can be matched by configuration: each of the 224 pairs in
Table~\ref{tab:improvers30} has the same constructor, strategy, network and
demand realisation, with a different configured improver. This controls the
scenario mix, but it is not a controlled treatment comparison. Collected
tonnage or bin count differs in many pairs, showing that stochastic upstream
construction did not reproduce the same input route for both improvers.

In aggregate, configurations using CLS have higher efficiency than their
matched-demand Fast-TSP counterparts (Table~\ref{tab:improvers30}). CLS has a
broader neighbourhood that can move bins between routes, whereas Fast-TSP holds
each route's bin assignment fixed and changes only its internal order, but the
observed delta cannot be attributed to that difference alone.

The losses are concentrated. Twenty-one of the
22 CLS losses occur at $N=350$; the remaining loss is HGS at $N=170$. Every
loss belongs to PSOMA, SWC-TCF, or HGS, and the negative differences are fewer
but deeper than the positive ones (Fig.~\ref{fig:improver}). This concentration
motivates a controlled rerun at $N=350$ before selecting an improver for those
constructors. The different neighbourhoods provide a plausible mechanism, but
the summary logs contain no controlled route-length intervention with which to
identify it.

Overflow counts tie on 181 of the 224 pairs. The remaining differences are
further evidence that the stored experiment did not hold every upstream outcome
fixed across improvers.

\subsubsection{Effect of the Planning Horizon}
\label{sec:res-horizon}

Table~\ref{tab:horizon} compares the 165 configurations observed at both
horizons against themselves. Efficiency is essentially horizon-invariant and
slightly improving: the mean paired change is $+0.26$\,kg/km, positive in 140 of
165 pairs, with HGS the only constructor whose efficiency falls ($-0.47$). For
this selected subset, the result is consistent with efficiency being governed
more by route geometry than by horizon length; it is not evidence for that claim
over the unobserved policies.

Overflow counts grow, and the magnitude is informative: among pairs with a
nonzero 30-day count, the median 90-day to 30-day ratio lies between 2.4 and 3.3
for six of the seven constructors, against a horizon ratio of exactly 3.
The observed overflow counts therefore grow approximately in proportion to the
horizon for these six constructors. SWC-TCF is the exception at 5.6, consistent
with its scaling exposure elsewhere in these results.

Two cautions apply. First, this comparison is paired precisely because a
cross-constructor comparison at 90 days is not available. Second, pairing does
not undo selection into the 90-day sample: every configuration was carried
forward because of its 30-day Pareto performance. The table therefore describes
the selected configurations only. With one realisation per configuration, we
cannot estimate the selection effect or claim that the observed direction would
hold over the full factorial design.

\subsubsection{Scenario Effects}

The two generating processes produce markedly different regimes and should not
be pooled (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios30}). On policy slices present under both
processes, Gamma-3 produces both higher collection efficiency and more overflow
events than the Empirical process. It is a heavier load: more waste is available
per kilometre, but capacity is reached more often.

Network size does not act monotonically on efficiency because the two cities
differ in spatial density as well as scale. Cost does increase across the three
networks: both mean distance and runtime are highest at $N=350$, as are overflow
events. These marginals are restricted to policy slices shared by all levels of
the factor being compared; without that balancing, the exclusions confined to
Gamma-3 at $N=350$ would confound the scenario contrasts.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
WSmartRoute+ makes the temporal state of smart waste collection part of the
benchmark rather than an implementation detail. Its three-stage policy model
separates the decision to collect from the decisions that construct and refine a
route, while common demand realisations make comparisons within a scenario
directly attributable to policy behaviour. This separation exposed the central
operational result of the study: across the tested design, changing mandatory
selection moves the efficiency--service trade-off more than changing the route
constructor. Constructor choice still matters, particularly in runtime and in
the tails of the overflow distribution. Improver configurations are associated
with markedly different outcomes, but the stored runs do not isolate that stage
from stochastic upstream construction.

The evaluation also shows why integrity rules belong in the experimental
method. Whole-cell exclusion prevents failed SWC-TCF runs from flattering that
constructor, and balancing every reported marginal prevents the same exclusions
from flattering a strategy or scenario level instead. The 90-day results require
an equally explicit boundary: because follow-up was conditioned on 30-day
Pareto membership, they describe matched selected policies and cannot support a
cross-constructor ranking or a population-level horizon effect.

The immediate next experiment is therefore a complete, replicated 90-day
factorial design with multiple random seeds. That design would separate horizon
effects from selection and regression to the mean. Further work should exercise
the framework's learned constructors against the classical baseline reported
here, extend evaluation to multi-vehicle and multi-depot operations, and test
whether adaptive selection rules can move the observed efficiency--service
frontier rather than merely choose a point along it. For the monolithic
two-commodity-flow model, explicit timeout and completion telemetry should also
replace post-hoc failure detection at the largest scale.
